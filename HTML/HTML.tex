\chapter*{Preface}

These are my notes for \emph{Machine Learning} (機器學習), taught by Hsuan-Tien
Lin (林軒田) at National Taiwan University in the fall of 2026. This preface
reproduces the course-introduction handout of 09/09/2026, so that the ground
rules sit next to the material they govern; everything after it is the course
itself.

\section*{Five Reasons for NOT Taking the Course}

The course opens, by tradition, with an argument against taking it.

\begin{description}
    \item[Complicated contents.] From a Taiwanese student taking the MIT ML
        class (translated):
        \begin{quote}\itshape
            The professor started writing math equations as if he was using some
            writing accelerator. After class I always felt feeble. The worst part
            is: I needed to understand the contents as soon as I can. Otherwise I
            cannot finish the homework and cannot follow up in the next class.
        \end{quote}
        The NTU ML class is designed to be as good as the best classes in the
        world, so similar things will happen to you. \emph{If you are not willing
        to be so miserable, \ldots}

    \item[Strict instructor.] Will you give me a second chance if I copy homework
        from other people? No. Could you let me pass because I will be kicked out
        by the 1/2 rule? No. Will you change my score from F to C? No. How many
        will pass? Any, if necessary. \emph{If you do not like a strict
        instructor, \ldots}

    \item[Huge loads.] From a student taking ML 2010, posted on BBS
        (translated):
        \begin{quote}\itshape
            Only 1 problem requires running 100 test experiments?
            (\(100 \times 10\) min \(= 16\) hr); I double-checked multiple
            times --- the one who designed this homework is heartless.
        \end{quote}
        This class is two to six times harder than a normal one at NTU: six
        homework sets are planned, plus the final project, each due within two
        weeks in general. Homework~0 arrives in the first week --- already hard,
        worth very few points, and there to remind you of the prerequisites.
        \emph{If you do not want to spend so much time on homework, \ldots}

    \item[Online-learnable.] Two of the course's incarnations were invited by NTU
        as Massive Online Open Courses on NTU-Coursera, \emph{Machine Learning
        Foundations} and \emph{Machine Learning Techniques}, which cover
        \(70\%\) of what we teach; it is much easier to simply take the recorded
        version at home. Indeed we will do pseudo-flipped teaching. \emph{If you
        want to learn at your own pace, \ldots}

    \item[Shallow and old-fashioned.] This is not a deep learning class --- it
        surrounds shallow (old) machine learning. Yes, many of the materials are
        ten or more years old; and yes, AI has knowledge of those materials, just
        as AI can write code. \emph{If you want to chase newer buzzwords,
        \ldots}
\end{description}

From a student in ML 2013, in the final feedback (translated): \emph{``this is a
class that makes you experience all kinds of devastation and desperation.''}

\begin{moral}
    May the Brave Ones Stay.
\end{moral}

\section*{The Course and Its History}

\begin{itemize}
    \item 2008--2012: 3 credits, taught in English.
    \item 2013--2014: 3 credits, Mandarin, with MOOC.
    \item 2015: 4 credits, Mandarin, with MOOC.
    \item 2016--2020: mostly \(2+2\) credits (MLF \(+\) MLT), Mandarin, with
        MOOC.
    \item 2021 Fall and 2023 Spring: 3 credits, English.
    \item 2023 Fall: 3 credits, Mandarin, with MOOC offline and physical
        discussions stimulated by ChatGPT.
    \item 2024: 3 credits, English, TAICA.
    \item 2026: 3 credits, Mandarin, TAICA, with MOOC offline and discussions
        stimulated by ChatGPT.
\end{itemize}

The reason for moving the lectures offline is that many students prefer watching
a recording on their own time, which frees the precious class time to encourage
speculation rather than lecturing. Three things are new this semester.

\begin{description}
    \item[Cross-university teaching (2nd time).] Taiwan needs more AI talent, so
        the course accepted an invitation from TAICA (Taiwan AI College Alliance)
        to be offered to 50\(+\) universities --- with thanks to the MOE for
        more TAs, a YouTube screencast to break the time and space boundary, and
        some online TA hours to be planned. An attempt to further increase the
        impact in this new AI age.
    \item[Final exam.] AI can solve all of your homework with a high success
        rate. That is not a totally bad thing for teaching, but it makes fair
        grading exponentially difficult, so we will experiment with a
        closed-book final exam bearing some similarity to the homework --- an
        old-fashioned yet fair grading protocol, until something better is
        designed.
    \item[AI-assisted TAship.] AI can answer many questions with \(90\%+\)
        correctness in a friendly manner; the question is how to best help
        learning. We are building an AI agent to answer students' questions,
        guarded by the TAs' regular human checking --- human-computer
        collaboration towards better teaching and learning.
\end{description}

On enrollment: per school policy, students who have taken another version of the
course (ML Foundations, ML Techniques, or the 4-credit ML) are generally
\emph{not} allowed to take it again. We will not check, but the school may, and
multiple takings may not look nice on a transcript anyway. NTU students sign up
through the link on the course infopage, processed progressively; a limited
quota is reserved for TAICA students from other universities. Auditing is
welcome in general, as long as it does not interfere with official members.
\emph{Leave as soon as possible --- give your classmates a chance to be
miserable.}

\section*{THE Book}

\begin{center}
    \emph{Learning from Data: A Short Course}\\
    Y.~Abu-Mostafa (Caltech), M.~Magdon-Ismail (RPI), H.-T.~Lin (NTU)
\end{center}

The idea was initiated in 2008. Its five chapters are closely needed for the
first part of the class, and further e-Chapters are used in the second part; the
teaching follows the book, with suggested reading inside it. Copies can be found
at the NTU Library (a reserved copy in the shared course material area) and in
R536 (shared copies to be read in the room, if open); the Chuan-Hwa Book Company
has imported some copies, and Amazon/Kindle is the main selling channel in the
US. If the book is not affordable to you but you really want to read it, email
the instructor and he will see how he can help.

\section*{THE Principle}

Taking any unfair advantage over other class members is not allowed. It is
everyone's responsibility to maximize the level of fairness.

\begin{moral}
    \begin{center}
        NO CHEATING \\ NO LYING \\ NO PLAGIARISM \\ NO PIRATING of THE BOOK
    \end{center}
\end{moral}

There are very serious consequences. In practice this means: homework discussions
are encouraged, but write the final solutions independently and understand them
fully; references --- books, notes, the Internet, AI tools --- can be consulted
but not copied from, and you are strongly encouraged to save your AI prompts and
interaction logs. There is no need to lend, borrow, sell, buy or trade a
solution, a piece of code or a dialogue, and doing so is not allowed.

\section*{Grading}

There is no midterm. The main reference is
\begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
        \toprule
        homework sets & \(30\%\)\\
        final exam    & \(30\%\)\\
        final project & \(40\%\)\\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}
after which the raw score goes through an order-preserving normalization: a raw
score of \(80\) with term rank A is possible, and so is a raw score of \(60\)
with term rank F; raw scores \(80\) and \(60\) both mapping to B is possible but
unlikely; raw scores \(80\) and \(60\) mapping to F and B respectively is
impossible. For non-NTU students the rank will tentatively be converted by the
NTU metric back to a score in \([0, 100]\), which the student's own university
then converts back to a rank.

\emph{Homework} uses multiple-choice problems for auto-grading, with photographed
or scanned details and code uploaded for selective grading by the TAs --- there
is a penalty if your details are not logical --- and for plagiarism detection.
Late parts are worth \(90\%\) if twelve hours late, \(80\%\) if one day late, and
so on; each person is granted four penalty-free late half-days, the gold medals.
About a quarter to a third of the problems are programming assignments, in any
language, on any platform, with any packages; AI-generated code is fine if you
co-wrote it with the AI and your logs justify that.

The \emph{final project} is a teamed in-class competition, with team size to be
decided, co-run with Prof.\ Shou-de Lin's \emph{Machine Learning: Theory and
Practice} class --- so the two classes cannot both be taken this semester. You
will need to sign and abide by a data policy. It can make you rich, if you know
what I mean.
